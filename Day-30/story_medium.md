# Predicting hospital strain is easy. Acting early is the hard part.

Hospitals don’t run on averages — they run on thresholds. A few percentage points of utilization can be the difference between a normal day and a night of diversion calls, delayed admissions, and staff burnout. So for this final build in my 30-days-of-data-science sprint, I treated hospital capacity forecasting as an operations product: forecast inpatient and ICU occupancy, quantify uncertainty, and translate those forecasts into early-warning surge alerts with concrete staffing and transfer triggers.

The pipeline is designed the way a real ops analytics system should be: reproducible, contract-driven, and auditable. I ingest HHS Protect capacity time-series into DuckDB, enforce a forecasting “contract” (grain, targets, horizons, covariates), run automated data-quality gates, and produce a gold panel with engineered time-series features. From there, I backtest strong baselines (naïve/seasonal/EMA) and compare them to main models that learn from lags and seasonal structure. The key shift is probabilistic forecasting: instead of a single point estimate, the system produces prediction intervals and, crucially, the probability that utilization will cross an operational threshold in 1, 7, or 14 days.

That probability becomes the decision layer. If the risk of crossing an ICU or inpatient utilization threshold exceeds a trigger probability, the system emits an alert with an action: activate surge staffing, prep inter-facility transfers, open flex capacity, or coordinate elective scheduling. A simple cost model makes the tradeoff explicit: false alarms waste attention, but late alerts can be far more expensive in delays and avoidable acute events. The result is a dashboard-ready table that an ops team can consume immediately — not just “what will happen,” but “what should we do next, and how confident are we?”

If you’re building forecasting for real-world decisions, this is the lesson: the model is only half the story. The other half is contracts, quality gates, uncertainty, and an action layer that respects constraints. That’s how forecasting becomes operations intelligence.
